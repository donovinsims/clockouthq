# Decide whether a service area needs its own page

**Topics** · local-seo, content-quality, service-area-pages

## When to use it

Use when a lead serves multiple places and you are deciding what to include in a website brief or future content plan. Apply the guide to the current brief and use the evidence and access available for that task.

## Inputs

Confirmed areas served, related pages, the visitor’s location-specific needs, and verifiable local evidence such as coverage constraints, projects, or actual booking details.

## Steps

1. Verify the business serves the area and record any meaningful limitations, such as scheduling or coverage boundaries.
2. List questions a visitor from this area would ask. Identify the facts that make the answer different from the main service page or a neighboring area page.
3. Map unique facts to useful sections, related services, and the appropriate next action. Keep private customer details out of the brief.
4. Recommend a separate URL only if it has a distinct visitor purpose and enough verifiable information. Otherwise recommend a concise coverage section on an existing page.

## Decision rules

Treat maps, projects, and local examples as optional evidence to use when accurate. When proof is missing, request it or use the existing page. The post supplies a quality principle and gives no outcome evidence. A lead review can produce findings and a brief using public or authorized material. Apply changes within the project’s authorized scope.

## Expected output

A short recommendation for each area with its visitor need, unique evidence, and proposed destination.

## Verification

Compare the proposed brief with a nearby area page. If the only differences are place names and generic wording, reconsider the separate URL.

## Example

A contractor serves two adjacent towns with identical coverage and booking. List both on the main page. If one town has a documented service constraint and several questions specific to it, assess whether those details justify a separate brief.
