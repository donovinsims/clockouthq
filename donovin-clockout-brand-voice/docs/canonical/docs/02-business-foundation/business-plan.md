## Customer and problem
Start with owner-led local businesses and independent operators in northern Illinois/Stateline. Look for repeated valuable problems, decision-maker access, willingness to act, and feasible permissions. Local geography provides access and credibility; it is not a permanent geographic restriction.

Potential pains include missed inquiries, slow follow-up, repeated administration, disconnected tools, owner-dependent scheduling and poor visibility into work. These are hypotheses until a particular owner's workflow and baseline confirm them.

## Value creation
Audit the existing process and its economics. Remove unnecessary steps. Improve responsibilities and handoffs. Automate only where the resulting process warrants it. Use the customer's existing tools before adding software. Maintain human control over consequential commitments.

Measure outcomes such as response time, unanswered requests, follow-up completion, errors, work completed, and owner effort. Do not equate every saved hour with a cash saving.

## Segment selection and local competition

Use one simple comparison sheet per research batch. Columns: segment, recent problem example, reachable buyer, frequency, credible cost or consequence, budget evidence, existing alternatives, implementation/access risk and repeat potential. Rank supported evidence above an attractive industry label. Start with the established Stateline communities; proximity can help conversations and onsite assessment, but is not proof of demand. Do not assume every local owner wants AI.

For each serious opportunity compare the customer's actual alternatives: do nothing, change staff responsibilities, configure existing software, hire their current provider, or commission Clockout. Research a few locally relevant providers from their own public sites and record date, scope and positioning, not invented sales volumes. Ask the buyer what they already considered. Differentiate through a clear diagnosis, small relevant proof, understandable scope, responsible implementation and handoff; test whether those factors matter in real buying decisions. A competitor with a broader portfolio is not evidence Clockout cannot help; a problem already adequately solved is a reason to decline.

Buying signals include a recent unresolved incident, an owner spending time on repeated follow-up, a deadline with real consequences, and willingness to supply evidence and approve a bounded intervention. Disqualifiers include no identifiable problem, no authority or permission, harmful use, dependencies beyond current ability, economics that cannot cover delivery, and expectations of guaranteed sales or unlimited support.

## Unit economics and capacity decisions

Before a quote, estimate discovery, build/configuration, QA, revision, communication and handoff hours separately. Add third-party and subcontract costs, support obligations and a contingency based on identified uncertainty. Choose a founder compensation assumption for the internal calculation; it is not a public hourly rate. Minimum sustainable price scenario = direct costs + estimated hours × internal compensation assumption + allocated overhead + contingency. Compare with the customer's supported value and alternatives. If the two do not meet, reduce scope transparently or decline; do not make a large unsupported ROI claim to bridge the gap.

**Illustrative capacity scenario:** 20 working hours/week, less 5 for sales/admin and 3 for existing support, leaves 12 delivery hours. A site estimated at 10 hours fits one such weekly slot with little slack; two deposits do not create a second slot. If the work takes 16 hours, either the promised date or other commitments must change by agreement. These figures are planning examples, not Donovin's actual availability.

**Illustrative monthly scenarios:** two $500 projects produce $1,000 gross billed; eight produce $4,000 gross billed. At ten hours each, eight require 80 delivery hours before sales/admin, revisions and support. Neither scenario proves demand, settlement, profit or achievement of the $5,000 goal. A $20,000 target requires a different validated combination of scope, price, recurring responsibility or capacity; it cannot be reached by assuming conversion or ignoring labor.

## Management rhythm and growth gates

Weekly, review the pipeline, accepted obligations, cash runway, project contribution and hours. Decide the next concrete work item before starting another tool experiment. Monthly, compare estimates with actual hours, paid demand, customer outcomes, rework and support. Keep a service if it solves a recurring verified problem and can be delivered profitably; revise or stop when evidence disagrees. Use the finance manual's definitions so billed revenue, collected receipts and available cash are not interchanged.

Standardize a service only after several comparable deliveries reveal common inputs, acceptance criteria and exceptions. Document a reusable checklist before creating a software product. Consider a contractor only when a bounded repeatable task, sufficient margin, permissions, quality review and access controls exist. Consider recurring service only when recurring work, measurable responsibility and support capacity exist. Hiring is not a scheduled milestone or a substitute for finding demand.
