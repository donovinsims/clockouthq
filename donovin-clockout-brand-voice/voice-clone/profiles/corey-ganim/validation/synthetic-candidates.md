# Voice candidates for an independent blind test

All drafts are synthetic and **not** authored or endorsed by Corey Ganim. The real posts are held out of retrieval but included in the corpus for evaluation purposes. The writer cannot serve as an independent blind judge.

## Synthetic candidate 01 (long)

There is a very simple AI service hiding inside every owner-operated business with an unanswered inbox.

You don't need a custom agent that can run the whole company.

You need one job:

Find a new inquiry. Figure out what the person wants. Draft the response. Make sure the owner sees it before the lead goes cold.

The implementation:

1. Collect the actual inquiries. Not made up examples. Pull the last month and group them by request type.

2. Identify which requests are safe to answer with a template and which need an owner.

3. Give the system access to approved business information. Services, service area, working hours, pricing rules and the correct booking link.

4. Have it draft responses for approval first. Don't give it permission to make promises, discounts or commitments it can't verify.

5. Log each inquiry, response, time to first reply and final outcome.

6. Review the exceptions every week. Add rules for the ones that repeat.

The first version isn't supposed to be impressive. It's supposed to be trustworthy.

Once it works, you can expand the job to follow-up and scheduling.

A business owner doesn't need to understand the underlying model to understand why fewer leads sitting unanswered is valuable.

## Synthetic candidate 02 (long)

if you're building an AI service business and don't have clients yet, your portfolio probably shouldn't be another collection of generic demos.

Pick a real business with a visible problem.

Document what is broken. Show the exact workflow that would improve it. Explain what you'd need from the owner before you could deploy anything.

One useful piece of evidence beats a folder full of bots that solve nothing.

## Synthetic candidate 07 (long)

The easiest way to build an AI service you can sell more than once:

Stop starting with the software.

Start with the job.

Write down:

-What triggers it
-What the owner does today
-Where information comes from
-Who approves the final result
-What happens when the normal process breaks
-How you'll prove it improved

Then make one version work end to end.

If you need a different stack and a different custom integration for every customer, you don't have an offer you can repeat. You have an endless collection of one-off projects.

## Synthetic candidate 09 (long)

A lot of people are trying to sell AI automation to companies that haven't documented how work actually happens.

I'd approach it the other way around.

First, ask the person doing the work to walk through a real example. Write down every tool, decision and handoff.

Next, find the part that creates the most delays or rework. It might be a missing form field. It might be a messy approval rule. It might be someone retyping the same information into two systems.

Fix the process with the simplest possible change.

Only then decide whether an AI agent belongs anywhere in the workflow.

This is less fun than showing someone a bot with a hundred integrations.

But it's much easier to explain to the person paying the bill.

## Synthetic candidate 10 (long)

One of my favorite offers for a local AI operator is a process teardown.

Not a 50-page audit no one reads.

One workflow, documented with enough detail that the owner recognizes it.

Here's how I'd structure the deliverable:

The trigger:

What starts the work? New lead, service request, invoice, scheduling conflict, customer question.

The current process:

Every person involved, every tool, every handoff. If the same information gets entered three times, show all three.

The failure points:

Where requests get stuck, where customers wait, where the owner has to intervene, and where someone makes a decision without the right context.

The better process:

What should be removed, standardized or reassigned before adding any automation.

The first useful improvement:

The smallest change that would make tomorrow easier, even if it requires zero AI.

The automation candidate:

If the workflow still has a repetitive step, identify exactly what a bot could do, what it would need to read, and when a human has to approve.

The proof:

One simple metric to check after the change. Response time, rework, missed inquiries, hours spent or invoices collected. Use the business's real baseline.

The handoff:

Who owns it. What happens when it breaks. Which parts the client can edit. Which parts require support.

That's a much easier conversation than walking in and asking a business owner whether they want an AI agent.
