### 5. The gap

**The gap is real in positioning, unproven in demand.** Three things are simultaneously true:

1. **Three named providers serving the area publish a starting price a small owner can act on** (Waivium, CW Dynamic, Corporate Technologies). Everyone else — including both local AI providers — says "request a quote." Complaint #2 says owners resent exactly that.
2. **The one serious local AI capability sells above this buyer.** SparkFire's public logos are a bank, a law firm and Kraft Heinz; its published motion starts with a paid discovery sprint. Stateline AI Solutions is the nearest true rival and already speaks the "practical adoption for local business" language. Nobody local is visibly selling a **$1,500–$3,500 first project to a 5–25-employee owner with the price on the page**.
3. **The national productized offers prove a price point exists**, not that Rockford owners will pay it: Clear Function at $3,500 and Earley at $2,500 publish fixed assessment fees and both credit the fee forward.

**What Clockout can own, if it earns it:** *the operator who shows the work and the price first.* Specifically — a free look that names one bottleneck, a written fixed-scope implementation quoted in the $1,500–$3,500 band (to be set by the CFO work, not by this paragraph), client-owned accounts, one revision round, handover with the customer able to run it, and no retainer obligation to stay live. That combination answers complaints 2, 3, 4 and 5 directly, and it is the one thing no local competitor currently advertises.

**What is *not* a gap.** "AI for small business" is not differentiated — Stateline AI and Illinois AI Consultant already say it. Speed is not differentiated — CW Dynamic advertises 48-hour launches. Local presence is not differentiated — Jumping Trout has 29 years here and Bisconti 30. A $500 website is not differentiated on value — it is below every local price and reads as either a bargain or a warning.

**Honest counter-read.** If a buyer's problem is a bad website, CW Dynamic at $1,500–$5,000 with SEO and care plans is a better-matched purchase than a process consultant, and a $100/mo DIY stack is a rational answer for a two-person shop. The gap is narrow: it exists for owners with a *recurring operational* problem and enough volume to feel it. Finding out whether that owner exists locally is exactly what the consumer panel and real conversations test.

### 4. Historical proposal: the stack

**Name: The One Bottleneck Fix.** Clear, specific, and describes a job — not the company.

**The core.** One workflow, diagnosed and changed, for a fixed number written down before work starts: **$950 for one narrow fix**, **$2,500 for a whole workflow**. The outcome is stated as the thing that stops: quotes that never get a second call, calls that never get returned, a Monday spent re-typing what someone already typed.

**Bonuses** (each kills a named objection, each cheap):
1. **The One-Page Read** — one page on their own business, sent before any meeting, no meeting required. Kills 1, 4, 5, 6, 14. Cost: ~1.5 hours. *The single most important element in the stack.*
2. **The Written Test** — one page naming what "done" means, agreed before the first dollar moves. Kills 7, 8, 24. Cost: ~30 minutes.
3. **The Runbook** — a plain-language page with the change, how to run it, how to undo it in one step, and who to call. Kills 17, 22, 23. Cost: ~1 hour.
4. **The 30-Day Check** — one call, one month later, to see whether it held. Kills 21. Cost: ~30 minutes.

**The guarantee.** *If it does not pass the written test, the balance is not owed.* Not "money back": the balance is never collected until the test passes. The exposure is bounded and modelled in §5.

**Urgency and scarcity.** No countdown, no fake slots. The one true limit: **for the first three engagements, ask separately for permission to write up the result and arrange a reference call; permission is voluntary and never assumed** — a real trade, countable, and the exact asset the panel says is missing.

### 4. Routines (SOPs)

Each fits on one page and is meant to be followed on a bad day.

**SOP-1 Qualification (15 minutes per prospect).** Source and date recorded. Ask what happened the last time the problem occurred, what the owner did, what tools or people were involved, what remains unresolved, and what observable change would matter. Confirm owner/decision authority, access dependencies and willingness to continue. Record a short factual observation; do not infer a dollar saving. If the problem or access is unclear, do not quote yet.

**SOP-2 Optional One-Page Read (scoped, not automatic).** Prepare only after qualification when a concise written observation would help the owner decide. State what was reviewed, the observed bottleneck, the evidence source, a possible change, assumptions and next step. Do not invent a dollar value. The read is not required for every prospect and is not a substitute for investigation on uncertain work.

**SOP-3 The Written Test (30 minutes).** After investigation and before any implementation deposit: name the observable that proves it worked (calls returned within X hours, quotes followed up within 3 days, the report exists by Monday 8am). Name what is out of scope. Name who signs off. If the owner will not agree to a test, do not take the work.

**SOP-4 Access (15 minutes).** Minimum access, time-boxed. Client creates the account and grants access; Clockout never owns the domain, the phone system or the CRM. Revoke at handover. Write down what was accessed and when.

**SOP-5 Delivery.** Re-map the process before touching a tool. Change the process first. Configure existing software second. Automate last, and only the part with stable inputs and a human approval step for anything customer-facing. There is no automatic 18-hour qualification rule; the agreed scope and investigation determine the estimate.

**SOP-6 Acceptance (45 minutes, owner present).** Run the test on real work, not a demo. Record pass/fail per line. If it fails: fix or waive the balance — no arguing about what "pass" means, because it was written down.

**SOP-7 Handover (60 minutes).** The Runbook: what changed, how to run it, how to undo it in one step, who to call for what. Walk the owner's own person through it once. Confirm every account is in the client's name.

**SOP-8 The 30-day check (20 minutes).** One call: did it hold, what broke, what was never used. Write the answer down. This is the raw material for the first case study and the first reference.

**SOP-9 Complaint or incident.** Acknowledge the same day. Say what happens next and when. Fix first, explain second, never blame the tool. If it exposed customer data or sent a wrong message, stop the automation immediately and revert using the runbook.

**SOP-10 Weekly books (30 minutes).** Every engagement: hours, rework hours, direct costs, invoice status, money cleared. If a number is unknown, write "unknown," never zero.
