## 1. Why the first pitch failed

Baseline: **1 buy · 19 pass (5%)**, and the one "yes" was to the free hour, with zero purchases. The reasons, in the buyers' words:

| Blocker | Buyers | What it actually was |
|---|---:|---|
| Trust | 10 | Not "I don't like you" — "nobody I know has used you, and the last stranger who offered a free look was building a retainer" |
| Habit | 4 | "I already pay for software I barely use" and "I buy on referrals only" |
| Price | 3 | Not the amount — the *range*: "$1,500–$3,500 depending on what it takes is a range with a change order in it" |
| Timing | 1 | "Nothing is on fire badly enough this month" |
| Need | 1 | "I already know where my time goes" |

**The diagnosis.** The pitch asked a stranger to spend an hour on faith, then accept a variable number. It answered a question the buyer had not asked yet ("what would you fix?") before answering the one they had ("who are you, and what happens if this is wrong?"). Trust and price framing are hypotheses from the baseline, whose 19 passes included trust (10), habit (4), price (3), timing (1) and need (1). This was not a controlled price experiment and does not rule out willingness-to-pay problems.

## 4. Historical proposal: the stack

**Name: The One Bottleneck Fix.** Clear, specific, and describes a job — not the company.

**The core.** One workflow, diagnosed and changed, for a fixed number written down before work starts: **$950 for one narrow fix**, **$2,500 for a whole workflow**. The outcome is stated as the thing that stops: quotes that never get a second call, calls that never get returned, a Monday spent re-typing what someone already typed.

**Bonuses** (each kills a named objection, each cheap):
1. **The One-Page Read** — one page on their own business, sent before any meeting, no meeting required. Kills 1, 4, 5, 6, 14. Cost: ~1.5 hours. *The single most important element in the stack.*
2. **The Written Test** — one page naming what "done" means, agreed before the first dollar moves. Kills 7, 8, 24. Cost: ~30 minutes.
3. **The Runbook** — a plain-language page with the change, how to run it, how to undo it in one step, and who to call. Kills 17, 22, 23. Cost: ~1 hour.
4. **The 30-Day Check** — one call, one month later, to see whether it held. Kills 21. Cost: ~30 minutes.

**The guarantee.** *If it does not pass the written test, the balance is not owed.* Not "money back": the balance is never collected until the test passes. The exposure is bounded and modelled in §5.

**Urgency and scarcity.** No countdown, no fake slots. The one true limit: **for the first three engagements, ask separately for permission to write up the result and arrange a reference call; permission is voluntary and never assumed** — a real trade, countable, and the exact asset the panel says is missing.

**What it deliberately does not include:** a retainer, unlimited revisions, a website, a rebuild of their tools, a guaranteed number of new customers, or anyone else's results presented as Donovin's.

## 4A. What the two paid projects teach us

Donovin reports two completed paid projects: **$1,000** for a nail-salon booking experience intended to give the owner more control over scheduling, and **$2,000** for Pietro's Pizzeria in Roscoe to activate online ordering in Toast. The salon took about two elapsed weeks; the pizzeria work took about one elapsed month. These are owner-reported facts from November 4, 2026, not verified receipts or measured labor hours.

Both projects fit the broad implementation capability, but neither is evidence that every future job should use the $950/$2,500 ladder. The salon involved a custom booking experience; the pizzeria involved configuration of an existing POS platform. Their scopes, dependencies, risks and likely total hours differ. The price ladder remains a provisional comparison for bounded scopes, not a default quote for unknown custom work.

The operational lesson is specific: Donovin reports that he did not investigate the full scope before the first payment and discovered the size of the work after starting. The next offer therefore qualifies the problem, maps dependencies and records exclusions before an implementation deposit. If substantial uncertainty remains, an optional paid investigation may be proposed; it is not mandatory for a simple, well-understood fix and has no adopted price in this document.

Do not claim avoided platform fees, revenue impact, profit impact or customer outcomes from either project unless the owner, records and permission support the claim. A factual case note may describe the starting problem, work performed, ownership/handover and the client's words. A testimonial, reference call or public case study requires the client's explicit permission.

|---|---:|---:|

| Passes citing price | 3 | 2 |

The baseline is preserved unchanged in `panel-baseline/`; the re-test is `panel/`.

**What moved.** The offer now gets *read*: every one of the 20 said the free one-page read was acceptable, where the old free hour was treated as a trap. Two buyers who had been burned by an agency flipped to yes, which is the single most useful change — that objection was the one this review thought was unfixable by wording. Two of the four buys explicitly said they would start with the **$950** rung, not the $2,500 one, which supports keeping the small rung as the front door.

**What did not move.** The referral-only buyers still refuse for the same reason: nobody they know has used Donovin. Property managers (0/3) and AI skeptics (0/2) did not move at all. The trust count barely fell (10 → 9) because the objection changed shape rather than going away: "I would read your page" is not "I would pay you."

**One measured statistic is unusable.** Two buyers entered a dollar amount in the `purchases_first_month` field ($950 and $250), so the tally's "300.2 purchases per buyer" is nonsense. All answers are kept exactly as the buyers wrote them — nothing was edited — but the purchases figure is **excluded from every downstream calculation**, and the CFO ramp does not use it.

**Method caveat.** Two of the twenty re-test agents (P005, P020) performed web searches despite the brief's instruction, and P005 disclosed it. Their JSON answers do not cite web facts and are filed as written, but this is a deviation from the brief and is recorded in the checkpoint.

**Read this honestly: 20% is still a fail.** The compiler's default bar is 25%, and these are simulated buyers with unmeasured predictive accuracy. Trust remains the most frequent categorized refusal (9/16), alongside habit (4), price (2) and timing (1). The change suggests hypotheses to test, not proven conversion improvement.

## 8. What still has to become true

- **A reference he can name.** Until one real client says "call me," the trust objection is answered only with words. This is a prominent simulated objection; whether it is the binding real-world constraint is unverified.
- **The One-Page Read must contain a number the owner did not know** — otherwise objection 14 ("I already know what's broken") stands.
- **Donovin must tailor and agree the written test for each scope.** A blank template can help, but acceptance criteria and any revisions must be explicit before payment.
- **No public guarantee until at least one paid pilot has been delivered and its acceptance, hours, rework and support are recorded, the wording is reviewed, and Donovin approves publication.** One engagement does not establish a reliable claim rate; keep the 10% provision labelled as an assumption.

Rules observed: no invented reviews, testimonials, scarcity or discounts; every promise in the stack is deliverable within the modelled hours; guarantee wording is a commercial term to be checked by an adviser before use, not legal advice.
