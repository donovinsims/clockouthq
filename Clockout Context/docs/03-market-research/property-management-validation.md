## Hypothesis
Operationally busy residential property managers in the Stateline area may pay Clockout to reduce repetitive coordination and follow-through problems. Property management is the first focused validation hypothesis, not a proven specialization or a completed niche decision.

The earlier discussion prioritized property managers over transaction-driven real estate agents because recurring work may support repeatable improvements. Validate this locally rather than treating general industry research as buyer demand.

## Candidate workflows
Maintenance intake completeness, status communication, vendor follow-up, overdue work, owner reporting and lease-renewal administration. Choose one based on interviews. Do not assume a firm is broken because it operates in this industry.

## Proposed 30-day experiment
**Week 1:** Research 10–15 local firms. Record portfolio/team indicators only when publicly supported, existing systems if actually visible, owner access, one hypothesis and its evidence. Rank a few reachable fits.

**Week 2:** Personally approach the best five. Use the existing free-look entry. Interview owners about how a real request moves, where delays occur, frequency, consequences, current fixes and willingness to act. Record disconfirming evidence.

**Week 3:** Scope one paid pilot when demand exists. Baseline the workflow, define inputs, owner approvals, acceptance, fee, access, support and rollback. Configure existing tools where possible.

**Week 4:** Deliver and measure the agreed workflow. Compare baseline and result. Ask whether the buyer would pay again or refer another firm. Document the result with permission; do not imply success in advance.

These are proposed targets, not claimed outreach volumes, interviews or sales.

## Example pilot — maintenance follow-through
Approved intake → structured summary and missing information → manager review/assignment → reminders and status updates → exception escalation → completion evidence.

Human managers retain authority over safety, spending, vendor selection and consequential resident decisions. Keep tenant information in approved client systems. This is a delivery design boundary, not a statement of legal compliance.

## Research and discovery design

This remains a founder-interest hypothesis (S13), gated by the actual sprint review and Donovin's decision (U02). Residential property managers may coordinate owners, residents, vendors and staff; company size, fee model, authority and software vary. Potential costs include staff follow-up, repeat inquiries and delays, but prevalence, dollar impact and buyer willingness to pay are unverified for Clockout's market. No vendor landscape or market size is asserted as current fact.

After authorization to start the experiment, build a small, diverse public-source list: firm name, geography, managed property type, apparent decision-maker, source/date, software only if verified, and a research question. Compare small owner-led firms and larger managed portfolios; do not infer unit count or purchasing authority. Ask for interviews using a neutral invitation, not a manufactured problem. For each interview ask: “Walk me through the last maintenance request from intake to close”; “Where did it wait, and who followed up?”; “What tools did you use?”; “What happens when an urgent request arrives?”; “What have you already tried?”; “How do you measure the result?”; “Who would approve a change and budget?” Ask for estimates and their basis, and record disconfirming cases. Do not collect tenant identifiers or sensitive records merely to research.

**Workflow map:** trigger → intake → triage → assignment → vendor → status → resident/owner update → close. Capture actual roles, timestamps, handoffs, existing controls, failure modes and volume from consented examples. Investigate existing software's current features and terms using the vendor's primary documentation and the client's actual configuration; verify export, integration, cost and permissions before recommending a replacement or add-on. A spreadsheet may be adequate.

**Pilot design:** choose one frequent, low-consequence step with a named manager, explicit consent and baseline. Example: draft a status summary for staff review, never autonomous decisions on tenant eligibility, safety, spending or vendor selection. Agree sample size, observation window, manual comparison, time saved definition, error and escalation thresholds, data handling, fee, ownership, rollback and stop condition before work. Count elapsed and active staff time separately, log missed/escalated cases and ask users whether work actually improved. An illustrative estimate of 20 requests × 3 minutes saved = 60 minutes per period is a hypothesis; verify volume and time, subtract new review work, and never claim realized savings without measurement.

**Decision:** founder selects thresholds before observing results; record evidence and choose proceed, revise or stop. Lack of access, low pain, no buying authority, inadequate economics, unacceptable error rate or weak willingness to pay are valid stop signals. A workshop may map a workflow with staff and a decision-maker; scope, payment, output and follow-up must be agreed, and it is not an established current service.

**Sensitive decisions and privacy:** keep housing data in client-approved systems, collect the minimum and restrict access. Human review is necessary but does not itself establish legal compliance. Housing decisions may implicate fair-housing and other laws. [HUD's archived 2024 screening guidance](https://archives.hud.gov/news/2024/FHEO_Guidance_on_Screening_of_Applicants_for_Rental_Housing.pdf) describes fair-housing risks in screening, including algorithmic tools; its current status and local rules should be checked with qualified counsel before a relevant engagement. Escalate safety issues immediately under the client's existing procedures; do not automate eligibility, adverse decisions or emergencies.

## Business models, roles and workflow hypotheses

This section is a research map to verify in each firm, not a claim about every manager. A third-party manager serves property owners under a management agreement; an owner-operator manages its own holdings; a larger multifamily organization may divide leasing, maintenance and accounting across specialist roles. Fees, portfolio mix, authority and margins vary. Ask how the specific firm earns revenue and pays for work rather than assuming a standard fee percentage.

| Workflow | Roles and handoffs to investigate | Observable friction | Lower-complexity first option |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maintenance | Resident → coordinator → authorized vendor → manager | Missing details, no assignment, stale status | Required intake and named owner in existing system |
| Resident communication | Resident → office → responsible staff | Repeated status questions, inconsistent updates | Agreed status definitions and update cadence |
| Vendor management | Manager → vendor → invoice approver | Unaccepted jobs, missing completion evidence | Assignment acknowledgement and close checklist |
| Leasing administration | Inquiry → leasing staff → authorized decision-maker | Duplicate entry, missed appointments | Existing scheduling and approved templates |
| Owner reporting | Operations/accounting → manager → owner | Reconciliation work, missing context | Standard report inputs and review cutoff |
| Repetitive administration | Staff → system → reviewer | Copy/paste, incomplete records | Validate fields and remove unnecessary handoffs |

Do not automate screening, accommodation, adverse housing decisions or emergency judgment as an initial pilot. A routine communication can still reveal sensitive information; access and review remain necessary.

## Maintenance Follow-Through System — bounded hypothesis

**Problem to test:** ordinary maintenance requests may wait between intake, assignment, acceptance and completion because status ownership is unclear. **Proposed intervention:** a reliable queue and reviewed follow-up, using the client's current software first. No claim is made that local firms have this problem or will pay Clockout to solve it.

1. Map current states and authority. Proposed ordinary states: received, needs information, manager reviewed, assigned, vendor acknowledged, scheduled, completed pending verification, closed, exception. The client defines urgency, emergency routing, spending and vendor rules.
2. For each request keep an existing system ID, received time, responsible staff member, status, next action/date and completion evidence. Do not build a parallel database of tenant identities without justification and permission.
3. Establish who checks the queue and what a missed deadline means. Draft reminders for staff approval before any automated external message. Avoid duplicate reminders by keeping a stable request ID and last-notification marker.
4. Test with synthetic records: incomplete intake, duplicate request, unacknowledged vendor, overdue action, completion without evidence, reopened job, unavailable system and urgent report. Urgent or ambiguous cases route to the client's designated human procedure immediately.
5. Pilot a limited ordinary request category. Compare with baseline and retain manual fallback. Stop external sends if records are stale, permissions fail, duplicate sends occur or an urgent case is misrouted. The manager retains spending, safety and consequential communication authority.
6. Review weekly: percentage with named owner and next action, overdue open actions, median assignment delay, reopened requests, duplicate/missed notifications and net staff time. Record sample size and period; do not hide exceptions behind an average.

**Illustrative baseline:** 30 ordinary requests, 12 missing next actions; pilot 30, 4 missing next actions. That is a comparison of completeness, not proof of causation or financial ROI. If five minutes per request were saved but three minutes of review added, net saving would be two minutes; verify both in actual work.
